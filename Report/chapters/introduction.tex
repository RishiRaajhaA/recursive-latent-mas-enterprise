\section{Background and Motivation}
Customer service, logistics fulfillment, warranty processing and inventory management are increasingly relying on autonomous decision-making systems.

These agents are more than just question answerers. They must call external APIs, look up company policies, decompose complex customer goals, verify relational database status, process transaction updates and make sure all final constraints are met.

Instead of producing an answer in one feed-forward pass, an agent would work iteratively. It is thinking. It chooses a tool. It sucks up what comes back.

The wrong tool, badly formatted parameters or a rushed assumption can snowball into policy violations or outright task failure. Modern multiagent systems avoid these errors by assigning specialized roles and using recursive reflection. The catch is conventional systems can only speak text. Agents report their intentions, execution logs, critiques and error messages to us as natural language strings.

To overcome these limitations, this work proposes multi-agent recursive architectures communicating directly in continuous latent space. Continuous hidden tensors can flow between the specialized agent roles through parameterized neural adapters. This allows deliberation to occur in the high dimensional internal geometry of the model itself.

\section{Problem Statement}
In complex enterprise workflows of multi-agent language systems, text-based reflection results in heavy token overhead, high inference delay and accumulated errors as tasks get harder. There is no literature on continuous latent-state communication in stateful, tool-driven environments, empirically or geometrically. This thesis describes the design, implementation, and testing of a Recursive Latent Multi-Agent System against traditional Multi-Round Text ReAct in an enterprise customer service domain. It asks whether recursive latent communication can enable more robust and less computationally expensive task completion. Also, it asks about stability of trajectories in bounded solution regions with no dimensional collapse.

\section{Project Objectives}

Third, I developed geometric manifold metrics to mathematically characterize trajectory stabilization. Third, we evaluated runtime failure diagnosis with predictive linear probing. \section{Summary of Contributions}
An empirical benchmark evaluation and geometric manifold characterisation of continuous latent deliberation in recursive multi-agent systems are presented in this thesis. The principal contributions are:
First, we show that Latent ReAct offers significant operational benefits on intricate multi-step workflows, increasing Hard-tier goal accuracy by almost five percentage points (50.8\% versus 45.9\%) while executing 26.1\% faster, producing 25.2\% fewer tokens, and lowering enterprise policy violations by 23.1\%. The mean goal fulfillment accuracy increases by 0.6 percentage points throughout recursion depths (50.9\% against 50.3\%) for the entire benchmark and by 2.0 percentage points at terminal depth (56.4\% against 54.4\%), in addition to a 3.3 percentage point descriptive disadvantage in the precision of single-step tool selection (bootstrap 95\% CI: $[-5.1\%, -1.5\%]$).
Second, we provide empirical evidence of representational trajectory stabilization: Latent ReAct stabilizes deliberation trajectories into localized solution regions without experiencing catastrophic dimensional collapse ($18.9 \to 15.2$ effective dimensions), achieving a 72\% step-wise drift contraction across the entire benchmark cohort ($p = 1.9 \times 10^{-20}$, maintaining a 70\% contraction with $p = 7.1 \times 10^{-15}$ under duplicate-filtered robustness testing).
Third, verbal critique passing results in high context overlap (CosSim > 0.998). We tackle this by continuous latent routing via neural adapters, keeping agent roles nearly orthogonal and functionally specialized (CosSim <= 0.087, Dij ~ 51.5). Finally, the failure analysis yields a null result after the multiple hypotheses correction. Global, role-averaged representations do not linearly predict downstream task success. So, for large systems, local, tool-specific population representations may be better predictors of execution reliability than geometry, and may warrant further study.

\section{Thesis Organization}
The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 surveys the literature on representation geometry, recursive self-reflection, multi-agent frameworks, and ReAct. Chapter 3 covers the system architecture, the neural adapters, and the experimental setup. Chapter 4 is on the geometric and error analysis of the latent representations. Chapter 5 deals with benchmark performance, complexity scaling and geometric visualizations. Main results, limitations and suggestions for future work are concluded in Chapter 6. Appendix A describes the design of relational database and the benchmark API tools.
